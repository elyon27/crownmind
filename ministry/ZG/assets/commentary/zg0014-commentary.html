The Gift of Another Day: A Theology of Gratitude, Consecrated Living, and Perpetual Worship in the Temple of God

A Theological Commentary

A TTU Seminary-Level Exegetical Commentary

Central Biblical Texts: Lamentations 3:22–23; Psalm 118:24; Romans 12:1–2; 1 Corinthians 6:19–20; Hebrews 13:15; Psalm 100:4; Colossians 3:17.
Theological Themes: Divine Providence, Grace, Gratitude, Christian Stewardship, Pneumatology, Temple Theology, Sanctification, Consecration, and Perpetual Worship.

The Prayer for Exposition
Father God, Thank You for this day. Thank You for giving me another opportunity to serve You. I will Praise You O Lord my God in Your Most Holy Temple. I will offer You prayers of Praise and Thanksgiving now and forever. Amen.

I. Introduction: The Sacred Significance of Another Day
The prayer before us expresses a profound theological understanding of human existence: every new day is a gift of divine providence, every breath is an opportunity for faithful service, and every moment of life may become an offering of worship to the living God.
Although expressed in simple devotional language, the prayer contains a remarkable concentration of biblical doctrines.

Its opening acknowledges God as the giver and sustainer of life. Its second movement recognizes human existence as an opportunity for service. Its third movement introduces the biblical theology of the temple, while its conclusion anticipates the eternal worship of God.

The prayer therefore moves through four interconnected theological realities:
1. Gratitude for life received through divine grace.
2. Consecration of human existence to the service of God.
3. The sanctification of the believer as a dwelling place of the Holy Spirit.
4. The continuity between earthly worship and eternal praise.
At its deepest level, this prayer is not simply an expression of appreciation for another sunrise.
It is a confession that human life belongs to God.

The believer awakens not merely to continue existing, but to live intentionally before the Creator.
The prayer reflects an important Christian conviction: the value of a day is not ultimately determined by the wealth accumulated, the recognition received, or the accomplishments achieved, but by the faithfulness with which one responds to God's grace.
To receive another day with thanksgiving is therefore to recognize both a gift and a responsibility.
Life is given freely by God, and the believer is invited to offer that life back to Him in love.

II. Exegetical Foundation: "Father God, Thank You for This Day"
A. Lamentations 3:22–23 — The Renewal of Divine Mercy
The prophet Jeremiah's lamenting tradition offers a foundational passage:
"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." — Lamentations 3:22–23, KJV

These verses emerge from a context of national devastation, suffering, and judgment.
The theological significance is particularly striking because the declaration of God's faithfulness does not arise from favorable circumstances.
It arises amid affliction.

The Hebrew word חֶסֶד (ḥesed) refers to steadfast love, covenant loyalty, or enduring mercy.
The plural expression רַחֲמִים (raḥamim) communicates compassion and tender mercy.
Together, these concepts establish the foundation of biblical hope.
God's mercy is not exhausted by yesterday's failures or yesterday's suffering.
His covenant faithfulness remains the basis of hope for His people.
The expression "new every morning" does not suggest that God changes His character daily.
Rather, it affirms that His enduring compassion continues to sustain His people through changing circumstances.

The believer who says, "Father God, thank You for this day," therefore acknowledges the continuing reality of divine mercy.
A new morning becomes a reminder that life is sustained by grace rather than guaranteed by human power.
B. Psalm 118:24 — The Day as a Divine Gift
The psalmist declares:
"This is the day which the LORD hath made; we will rejoice and be glad in it." — Psalm 118:24, KJV

Within its original literary setting, Psalm 118 celebrates God's deliverance and covenant faithfulness.
The immediate context includes the rejected stone becoming the cornerstone, a passage later applied to Jesus Christ in the New Testament.
Consequently, the verse carries a richer meaning than the general affirmation that God creates every day.
It celebrates God's decisive saving action.
Nevertheless, its language also provides a legitimate devotional pattern for receiving each day with gratitude.

The Hebrew verb גִּיל (gîl) expresses rejoicing or exultation.
Biblical rejoicing is not necessarily dependent upon the absence of hardship.
It arises from recognizing the goodness and faithfulness of God.
Thus, the Christian begins the day not by claiming control over its outcome, but by acknowledging the Lord who sustains life.
The new day is not a possession to be demanded; it is a gift to be received with thanksgiving.

III. The Theology of Divine Providence: God as the Sustainer of Human Existence
A. Acts 17:28 — Living and Moving in God
The Apostle Paul declares:
"For in him we live, and move, and have our being." — Acts 17:28, KJV

Speaking before the Areopagus in Athens, Paul explains humanity's dependence upon the Creator.
His declaration challenges the notion that human existence is autonomous or self-sustaining.
Life itself depends upon God.

Christian theology identifies this continuing divine activity with the doctrine of providence.
Providence refers to God's sustaining, governing, and directing relationship with creation.
The believer's gratitude for another day therefore rests upon the recognition that existence is continually dependent upon divine goodness.

This does not mean that every event occurring during the day should be interpreted as a direct revelation of God's particular intentions.
Scripture encourages trust in providence while also teaching humility concerning the mysteries of God's purposes.

The believer may confidently affirm God's sustaining presence without claiming exhaustive knowledge of why particular circumstances occur.

B. James 4:13–15 — The Humility of Human Planning
James warns against presumptuous confidence in tomorrow.
He teaches that human life is brief and that plans should be made in recognition of God's will.
The expression "if the Lord will" in James 4:15 reflects the proper attitude of Christian dependence.
This does not prohibit planning.
Rather, it places planning under divine sovereignty.

The prayer of thanksgiving for another day therefore encourages humility.
The believer recognizes that time is entrusted rather than owned.
Each day invites responsible action, but its ultimate outcome remains beyond complete human control.
This understanding produces neither fatalism nor inactivity.
It produces grateful stewardship.

IV. "Thank You for Giving Me Another Opportunity to Serve You": The Theology of Vocation
A. Service as the Purpose of Redeemed Life
The second movement of the prayer introduces an essential dimension of Christian discipleship.
Life is not received merely for personal enjoyment.
It is also an opportunity to love and serve God.
Ephesians 2:8–10 establishes the relationship between grace, salvation, and good works.
Paul teaches that salvation is God's gift, not the result of human works, and that believers are created in Christ Jesus for good works.

The theological order is important:
Grace precedes service; service responds to grace.
The believer does not serve God in order to purchase divine acceptance.
Rather, faithful service emerges from a life reconciled to God through Jesus Christ.

This distinction protects Christian spirituality from legalism.
Service is not a means of earning salvation.
It is an expression of gratitude for salvation received.

B. The Greek Concept of Service: Diakonia
The New Testament frequently uses the Greek noun διακονία (diakonia) to describe service or ministry.
The term may refer to practical assistance, ministerial responsibilities, or service performed for the benefit of others.

Its theological significance becomes especially clear in the ministry of Jesus Christ.
Mark 10:45 declares:
"For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many." — KJV

The Greek verb διακονῆσαι (diakonēsai) means "to serve."
Christ establishes the pattern of Christian service through His own self-giving life.
Consequently, the believer's opportunity to serve God must not be restricted to religious ceremonies or institutional ministries.

Service to God includes compassion toward others, honesty in one's responsibilities, care for the vulnerable, reconciliation, and faithful performance of ordinary duties.
The Christian vocation is fundamentally shaped by love.

C. The Sanctification of Ordinary Work
Colossians 3:17 declares:
"And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him." — KJV

This passage extends Christian devotion into the entirety of human conduct.
Work, relationships, responsibilities, and acts of kindness may become expressions of worship when undertaken in faith and love.

The prayer's reference to "another opportunity" therefore carries significant ethical meaning.
A day need not contain extraordinary achievements to possess spiritual value.
The ordinary acts of patience, forgiveness, diligence, and compassion can become meaningful expressions of service.

A life offered to God is sanctified not merely through extraordinary religious accomplishments, but through ordinary faithfulness sustained by grace.

V. "I Will Praise You, O Lord My God, in Your Most Holy Temple": Biblical Temple Theology
The third movement of the prayer introduces one of Scripture's richest theological themes: the temple as the place of divine presence and worship.

To interpret this statement responsibly, it is necessary to trace the development of temple theology throughout biblical revelation.

A. The Temple in the Old Testament
In ancient Israel, the tabernacle and later the Jerusalem temple represented the covenantal dwelling of God among His people.
Exodus 25:8 records God's instruction:
"And let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them." — KJV

The Hebrew term מִקְדָּשׁ (miqdāsh) refers to a sanctuary or holy place.
The tabernacle expressed the extraordinary theological truth that the holy God chose to dwell among His covenant people.
Later, Solomon's temple became the central location of Israel's sacrificial worship.
However, the temple never implied that God could be confined within a physical structure.
In 1 Kings 8:27, Solomon acknowledges that even the heavens cannot contain God.
The temple was therefore a divinely appointed place of covenantal encounter, not a limitation upon divine omnipresence.
B. Jesus Christ as the Fulfillment of Temple Theology
In John 2:19–21, Jesus speaks of the destruction and raising of the temple.
The evangelist explains that Jesus was referring to the temple of His body.
This passage is profoundly significant.
Christ Himself becomes the decisive locus of God's presence and redemptive encounter with humanity.
The temple's sacrificial and mediatorial significance finds its fulfillment in Him.
Through His death and resurrection, access to God is established through the mediation of Jesus Christ.
Hebrews 10:19–22 further develops this theology by describing believers' confidence to approach God through the blood of Jesus.
Christian worship is therefore fundamentally Christ-centered.
The believer does not require a particular earthly sanctuary to enter into communion with God.
Through Christ, access to the Father is granted by grace.

VI. The Believer as the Temple of the Holy Spirit
A. First Corinthians 6:19–20 — The Sanctity of the Human Person
Paul writes:
"What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?" — 1 Corinthians 6:19, KJV

The Greek word translated as temple is ναός (naos).
Unlike ἱερόν (hieron), which can designate the broader temple complex, naos commonly refers to the sanctuary itself.
Paul's choice emphasizes the sacred character of the believer's embodied life.
Within its immediate context, the passage addresses sexual holiness and the proper use of the body.
Its broader theological implication is that the Christian belongs to God.
The body is not spiritually insignificant.
It is part of the believer's redeemed existence.
The prayer's reference to praising God in His holy temple may therefore be understood, in a secondary devotional sense, as praise offered through a life indwelt by the Holy Spirit.
However, an important distinction must be preserved.
The biblical doctrine of the believer as God's temple does not imply that human beings become divine in essence.
Nor does it mean that personal feelings or thoughts automatically possess divine authority.
Rather, it means that God graciously dwells among and within His redeemed people through the Holy Spirit.
B. The Church as the Corporate Temple of God
First Corinthians 3:16–17 and Ephesians 2:19–22 extend temple imagery to the gathered community of believers.
In Ephesians 2, Jews and Gentiles are described as being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit.
This introduces an essential ecclesiological dimension.
Christian temple theology is not merely individualistic.
The Church is collectively called to embody holiness, reconciliation, and worship.
Therefore, the prayer's reference to the holy temple should not be interpreted exclusively as private spiritual experience.
It also points toward participation in the worshiping community of God's people.
The believer is called to worship God personally, while remaining united with the wider Body of Christ.

VII. The Theology of Praise: Worship as the Proper Response to Divine Goodness
A. Psalm 100:4 — Entering with Thanksgiving
The psalmist declares:
"Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise: be thankful unto him, and bless his name." — Psalm 100:4, KJV

Two important Hebrew terms appear within the theology of praise.
תּוֹדָה (todah) refers to thanksgiving or an expression of grateful praise.
תְּהִלָּה (tehillah) refers to praise.
These concepts are closely related but possess distinct emphases.
Thanksgiving acknowledges God's gracious acts.
Praise celebrates God's character, majesty, and worthiness.
The prayer under consideration includes both.
The believer thanks God for another day and praises Him as Lord.
This reveals the proper movement of worship.
The worshiper begins by acknowledging divine gifts but moves toward adoration of the divine Giver.
God is worthy of praise not only because of what He provides, but because of who He eternally is.
B. Hebrews 13:15 — The Sacrifice of Praise
The author of Hebrews writes:
"By him therefore let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name." — Hebrews 13:15, KJV

The Greek expression θυσίαν αἰνέσεως (thysian aineseōs) means "sacrifice of praise."
The language intentionally evokes the sacrificial worship of the Old Testament.
Yet Christian praise is offered through Jesus Christ.
It is not an additional atoning sacrifice.
Christ's redemptive offering is sufficient and complete.
Rather, the believer's praise is a grateful response to His finished work.
The concept of sacrifice also suggests that praise may be offered in difficult circumstances.
Thanksgiving is not restricted to moments of prosperity.
A believer may praise God while grieving, struggling, or waiting for deliverance.
Such worship does not deny suffering.
It acknowledges God's faithfulness in the midst of suffering.

VIII. Romans 12:1–2 — The Consecration of the Entire Life
Paul exhorts believers:
"Present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service." — Romans 12:1, KJV

The Greek expression λογικὴν λατρείαν (logikēn latreian) is variously rendered as reasonable, rational, or spiritual worship.
Paul draws upon sacrificial imagery to describe the Christian life.
Under the new covenant, believers are called to present themselves to God as living sacrifices.
This is not a call to self-destruction.
It is a summons to consecrated living.
The entire person—body, mind, will, and conduct—is offered in service to God.
The following verse commands transformation through the renewal of the mind.
Thus, Christian worship involves both inward renewal and outward obedience.
The prayer "I will offer You prayers of praise and thanksgiving" finds its fuller meaning within this Pauline framework.
Praise is not merely spoken.
It is embodied.
A life characterized by mercy, truthfulness, self-control, generosity, and faithfulness becomes a continuing expression of worship.
The highest expression of thanksgiving is not simply to thank God for life, but to live that life in a manner that honors Him.

IX. The Christological Dimension: Thanksgiving Through Jesus Christ
Christian thanksgiving is inseparable from the mediatorial work of Jesus Christ.
Colossians 3:17 explicitly directs believers to give thanks to God the Father through Him.
This establishes the Christological foundation of worship.
Christ is not merely an example of gratitude.
He is the mediator through whom believers approach the Father.
First Timothy 2:5 identifies Christ Jesus as the one mediator between God and humanity.
Through His incarnation, obedience, sacrificial death, and resurrection, Christ establishes the basis of reconciliation.
Consequently, the Christian does not approach God on the strength of personal righteousness.
The believer approaches through the grace of Jesus Christ.
This understanding protects the prayer from becoming an expression of spiritual self-sufficiency.
The believer's ability to serve, worship, and give thanks is itself sustained by divine grace.
Even the desire to praise God is a gift that should lead toward humility.

X. The Pneumatological Dimension: Prayer in the Holy Spirit
A. Romans 8:26 — The Spirit's Assistance in Human Weakness
Paul declares:
"Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought." — Romans 8:26, KJV

This passage reveals that Christian prayer is sustained by divine assistance.
The believer's communion with God does not depend upon eloquence, emotional intensity, or perfect understanding.
The Holy Spirit helps believers in their weakness.
The prayer of thanksgiving therefore becomes an expression of dependence upon the Spirit.
The Spirit guides believers toward the Father through Jesus Christ.
B. The Trinitarian Structure of Christian Worship
The prayer begins with the words "Father God."
This establishes its direction toward the Father.
Its theological foundation, however, is fully understood within the work of the Trinity.
The Father is the source of life and the recipient of worship.
The Son is the mediator of reconciliation and access.
The Holy Spirit enables the believer's communion with God and sanctifies the life offered in worship.
Christian prayer is therefore fundamentally Trinitarian:
To the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit.
This pattern preserves the unity of Christian worship while recognizing the distinct personal relations within the Godhead.

XI. "Now and Forever": The Eschatological Dimension of Praise
The concluding words of the prayer introduce an eternal horizon.
The believer offers praise not merely for the present moment, but "now and forever."
This declaration expresses the continuity between earthly worship and the eternal adoration of God.
A. Revelation 7:9–12 — The Worship of the Redeemed
John's apocalyptic vision presents a great multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and language standing before the throne and the Lamb.
Their worship celebrates salvation, blessing, glory, wisdom, thanksgiving, honor, power, and might belonging to God.
This scene reveals the universal scope of redemption.
Eternal worship is not restricted to one earthly nation, culture, or institution.
It is the worship of the redeemed gathered before God.
The believer's present thanksgiving anticipates this eschatological communion.
B. Revelation 21:22 — God and the Lamb as the Temple
One of the most remarkable statements in biblical temple theology appears in Revelation 21:22:
"And I saw no temple therein: for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it." — KJV

This verse brings the biblical theology of the temple to its consummation.
The sacred presence once represented by the tabernacle and temple is fulfilled in the immediate communion of God and His redeemed creation.
There is no need for a separate temple structure because God and the Lamb are present with His people.
This provides a profound eschatological interpretation of the prayer's reference to worshiping in God's most holy temple.
The believer worships now through faith, by the Spirit, and in union with Christ.
In the consummation of God's Kingdom, communion with God reaches its promised fullness.
Thus, the prayer moves from the gift of one earthly day toward the hope of eternal fellowship with God.

XII. The Ethical Implications of Daily Thanksgiving
A theology of gratitude must produce practical transformation.
Thanksgiving is not merely a religious sentiment.
It is a moral orientation that influences the believer's treatment of others.
A grateful heart recognizes life as a gift rather than an entitlement.
Such recognition should cultivate humility, generosity, compassion, and responsible stewardship.
The believer who thanks God for another opportunity to serve Him must consider how that opportunity will be exercised.
Will the day be used to practice forgiveness?
Will it become an occasion for reconciliation?
Will the believer speak truthfully, fulfill responsibilities, and show compassion toward those in need?
These questions connect devotional prayer with Christian ethics.
James 2:14–17 warns against a faith that remains disconnected from practical care for others.
Likewise, Matthew 25:31–46 emphasizes the significance of compassionate service toward those in need.
The believer's gratitude for another day therefore finds expression in the responsible use of that day.
Theological maturity requires the integration of prayer and action.
The hands that are raised in thanksgiving should also be willing to serve in love.

XIII. Spiritual Formation: The Daily Renewal of Consecration
The prayer may be understood as a pattern of daily consecration.
Each morning offers an opportunity to renew one's commitment to God.
Such renewal does not mean that salvation must be repeatedly earned or reestablished through human effort.
Rather, it reflects the ongoing process of sanctification.
The believer continually responds to God's grace through repentance, trust, obedience, and worship.
This pattern may be expressed through four spiritual movements.
First: Remembrance. The believer remembers that life is sustained by divine mercy.
Second: Thanksgiving. The believer responds to God's goodness with gratitude.
Third: Consecration. The believer offers the coming day's activities to God.
Fourth: Faithful Service. The believer seeks to express devotion through practical obedience and love.
These movements are not rigid religious requirements.
They are disciplines that can help cultivate attentiveness to God's presence and purposes.
The value of the practice lies not in its repetition alone, but in the sincerity and faithfulness it encourages.

XIV. Ecclesiological and Formational Implications for Tenio Theological University
Within the theological education and spiritual formation envisioned for Tenio Theological University, this prayer offers a comprehensive model of Christian discipleship.
Theological learning must extend beyond the acquisition of biblical information.
It must cultivate a life shaped by gratitude, humility, worship, and service.
A student may possess extensive theological knowledge yet remain spiritually immature if such knowledge does not produce love.
Conversely, sincere devotion requires biblical understanding so that faith remains grounded in truth.
The prayer provides a useful framework for integrating both dimensions.

Gratitude for life	Lamentations 3:22–23	Recognizing divine mercy
Humble dependence	James 4:13–15	Trusting God's providence
Consecrated service	Romans 12:1–2	Offering life in obedience
Temple holiness	1 Corinthians 6:19–20	Honoring God in embodied life

For TTU, the educational implication is clear.
Theological formation should produce believers who recognize each day as an opportunity for faithful discipleship.
Such formation must not encourage spiritual pride or claims of personal superiority.
Rather, it should cultivate humble servants whose lives bear witness to the grace of Jesus Christ.
The ultimate purpose of Christian theological education is not the exaltation of the student, teacher, or institution.
It is the glory of God.

XV. Theological Reflection: The Sacredness of Ordinary Existence
One of the most profound implications of the prayer is its affirmation of the spiritual significance of ordinary life.
The prayer does not begin with a request for wealth, success, influence, or extraordinary spiritual experiences.
It begins with thanksgiving for another day.
This simplicity is theologically meaningful.
Christian spirituality often discovers profound truths within ordinary realities.
A sunrise reminds the believer of creation.
A new morning recalls divine mercy.
Daily responsibilities become opportunities for service.
Prayer becomes an expression of communion.
And the passage of time reminds the believer of eternity.
The spiritual significance of a day is not determined by its outward magnificence.
A quiet day lived in faithfulness may possess greater moral and spiritual value than a day filled with public recognition.
The Christian tradition has long recognized the importance of ordinary faithfulness.
The call to holiness is not restricted to those who occupy visible religious offices.
It extends to all believers.
Every Christian is called to love God and neighbor within the circumstances of life.
Thus, the prayer becomes a declaration of humble purpose:
To receive life gratefully.
To live responsibly.
To serve faithfully.
And to return all praise to God.

XVI. Conclusion: From the Gift of a Morning to the Worship of Eternity
The prayer, "Father God, Thank You for this day. Thank You for giving me another opportunity to serve You," begins with the recognition of a simple gift: another day of life.
Yet its theological implications extend far beyond the present moment.
It acknowledges God as Creator and Sustainer.
It recognizes grace as the foundation of human existence.
It understands service as the proper response to divine goodness.
It identifies worship as the consecration of the entire life.
And it anticipates the eternal communion of the redeemed with God.
The movement from thanksgiving to service, from service to worship, and from worship to eternity reveals the unity of Christian existence.
The believer does not live one life for ordinary responsibilities and another for God.
Rather, the whole of life is to be lived before Him.
The prayer's reference to the holy temple reminds believers that God graciously dwells among His people through the Holy Spirit.
Its concluding promise of perpetual praise directs the heart toward the final fulfillment of redemption.
In this sense, the prayer is both a beginning and an anticipation.
It begins with the dawn of another earthly day.
It looks toward the everlasting light of God's presence.
The Christian awakens in gratitude because life is a gift.
The Christian serves because love calls for action.
The Christian worships because God alone is worthy.
And the Christian hopes because the promises of God find their fulfillment in Jesus Christ.

XVII. Final Theological Synthesis
The prayer may be expressed in a fuller theological confession:
O heavenly Father, Giver and Sustainer of all life, I thank You for the mercy that has brought me into another day. Let this day become an offering of gratitude, obedience, and love. Through Your Son, Jesus Christ, and by the sanctifying power of Your Holy Spirit, teach me to serve You faithfully in every responsibility entrusted to me. May my body, mind, heart, and soul be consecrated to Your glory. Receive my prayers of thanksgiving and praise, not only in the sanctuary of worship, but through the daily conduct of a life surrendered to Your will. And when the days of my earthly pilgrimage have reached their appointed end, may I share in the everlasting worship of Your redeemed people, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

The central theological principle may therefore be stated:
Every new day is a manifestation of divine mercy, every opportunity to serve is a calling to faithful stewardship, and every act of sincere thanksgiving anticipates the eternal worship of God.
The believer's life becomes an offering.
The heart becomes a place of prayer.
The body becomes an instrument of service.
And the Church becomes a community of praise.
All these realities find their foundation and fulfillment in Jesus Christ.
For the true measure of a life is not merely how many days have been granted, but how faithfully those days have been received and offered back to God.
Let every morning begin with gratitude. Let every responsibility become an opportunity for service. Let every act of love become an expression of worship. And let every day draw the soul nearer to the eternal presence of the Father.
Soli Deo Gloria — To God Alone Be the Glory.

TTU Exegetical Note: This commentary distinguishes the historical temple of Israel, Jesus Christ as the fulfillment of temple theology, the individual believer and the Church as dwelling places of the Holy Spirit, and the eschatological fulfillment described in Revelation 21:22. These related biblical images should not be treated as interchangeable in every context. The prayer is a devotional composition rather than a direct biblical quotation, and its theological interpretation is grounded in the cited scriptural passages.
