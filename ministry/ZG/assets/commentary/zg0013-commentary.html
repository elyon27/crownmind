The Joy of the Holy Spirit: Finding Our Highest Delight in Doing the Will of the Heavenly Father

A Theological Commentary

A TTU Seminary-Level Exegetical Commentary

Central Biblical Texts: Ephesians 5:18–20; John 4:34; Psalm 40:8; Romans 14:17; Galatians 5:22–25; Philippians 2:12–13.
Theological Themes: Pneumatology, Christology, Sanctification, Spiritual Joy, Divine Obedience, Christian Hedonism, and the Transformative Work of Grace.
Statement for Exposition:
"I get High in the Spirit of the Lord, and I get my kicks in doing the Will of My Father in Heaven."

I. Introduction: The Supreme Delight of a Spirit-Filled Life
At the heart of Christian spirituality lies a remarkable transformation: the movement of the human soul from seeking satisfaction in temporary pleasures toward finding its deepest joy in communion with the living God.

The statement under consideration employs unconventional, colloquial language to express a profound spiritual conviction. The expressions “get high” and “get my kicks” ordinarily refer to experiences of exhilaration, excitement, or pleasure. Within a Christian devotional context, however, these expressions may be understood metaphorically as describing the joy, spiritual vitality, and inward satisfaction associated with life in the Holy Spirit.

Theologically, the statement contains two interconnected declarations:
1. The experience of spiritual joy through the presence and activity of the Holy Spirit.
2. The discovery of profound satisfaction in fulfilling the will of the heavenly Father.

These two dimensions are not independent.
The Holy Spirit transforms the believer's affections so that obedience to God becomes increasingly desirable, rather than merely an obligation imposed from without.
This is one of the central realities of sanctification: God does not simply command obedience; through His grace, He renews the human heart so that obedience may become an expression of love.
The believer gradually discovers that the greatest spiritual satisfaction is not found in possessing the world but in belonging to God.

II. Exegetical Foundation: Being Filled with the Spirit
A. Ephesians 5:18 — The Contrast Between Intoxication and Spiritual Fullness
The Apostle Paul writes:
"And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit." — Ephesians 5:18, KJV

This passage establishes a significant contrast between two radically different forms of influence upon human conduct.
Paul contrasts intoxication with the fullness of the Holy Spirit.

The Greek expression:
πληροῦσθε ἐν πνεύματι (plērousthe en pneumati)
is commonly translated as "be filled with the Spirit."

The verb πληροῦσθε is a present passive imperative, conveying an ongoing command to receive and live under the Spirit's filling.

Three theological observations emerge.
First, the filling of the Spirit is divine in origin.
The believer does not manufacture the presence of the Holy Spirit through psychological excitement or emotional stimulation. Spiritual fullness is a work of divine grace.

Second, spiritual fullness is intended to characterize continuing Christian life.
Paul's command concerns the believer's ongoing existence under the influence and direction of the Holy Spirit.

Third, the Spirit's fullness produces transformed conduct.
The immediate context of Ephesians 5:19–21 associates Spirit-filled living with worship, thanksgiving, and mutual submission.
Therefore, the phrase "I get high in the Spirit of the Lord" must not be interpreted as spiritual intoxication in the literal sense.
Rather, it can express the profound joy of a person whose life is increasingly governed by God's Spirit.
Unlike chemical intoxication, which can impair judgment, the biblical fullness of the Spirit produces moral clarity, self-control, love, and wisdom.

B. The Difference Between Spiritual Joy and Emotional Exhilaration
A necessary theological distinction must be maintained between the emotional experience of joy and the abiding reality of the Holy Spirit.
Christian joy may involve powerful feelings of gratitude, peace, or exhilaration. Yet Scripture does not identify the presence of the Spirit exclusively with extraordinary emotional experiences.
Galatians 5:22–23 identifies the fruit of the Spirit as love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.

The Greek word for joy is χαρά (chara).
This joy is grounded in God's grace and the believer's relationship with Christ.
It may persist even when outward circumstances are difficult.
Consequently:
The fullness of the Holy Spirit is not measured by the intensity of spiritual excitement but by the transforming presence of Christlike character.
A believer may experience profound spiritual joy in quiet prayer, faithful labor, patient endurance, or compassionate service.
The deepest work of the Spirit is not always spectacular.
Frequently, it is revealed in the ordinary faithfulness of a transformed life.

III. Christological Foundation: Doing the Father's Will as Spiritual Nourishment
A. John 4:34 — The Food of the Son
Jesus declares:
"My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish his work." — John 4:34, KJV

This passage provides perhaps the most direct Christological foundation for the second half of the statement.
The Greek text reads:
ἐμὸν βρῶμά ἐστιν ἵνα ποιήσω τὸ θέλημα τοῦ πέμψαντός με.

A close rendering is:
"My food is to do the will of the One who sent Me."
The word βρῶμα (brōma) means food or nourishment.

Jesus uses physical nourishment as an analogy for His devotion to the Father's mission.
Just as food sustains physical life, obedience to the Father expresses the Son's deepest purpose.
This reveals an extraordinary theological truth:
For Jesus Christ, obedience to the Father was not an interruption of life; it was the very expression of His life and mission.

The believer's declaration of delight in doing the Father's will reflects this Christological pattern.
Nevertheless, the distinction between Christ and the believer must be preserved.
Christ's obedience is perfect, unique, and redemptive. The believer's obedience is derivative, imperfect, and made possible through grace.
Christ accomplishes salvation; believers respond to salvation through faith and obedience.

B. Psalm 40:8 — The Delight of Obedience
The psalmist declares:
"I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart." — Psalm 40:8, KJV

The Hebrew expression:
חָפַצְתִּי (ḥāphaṣtî)
comes from the verb חָפֵץ (ḥāphēṣ), meaning to delight in, desire, or take pleasure in something.
The psalmist does not merely acknowledge God's authority.
He expresses delight in God's will.

This distinction is foundational to biblical spirituality.
A person may obey a command reluctantly because of fear, social expectation, or external pressure.
But spiritual maturity increasingly involves obedience motivated by love.

The transformation is from:
"I must obey God" toward "I desire to obey God because I love Him."
These attitudes are not mutually exclusive. Reverent duty remains important, but grace deepens obedience into willing devotion.

Hebrews 10:5–10 subsequently interprets Psalm 40 christologically, presenting Christ as the One who perfectly fulfills the Father's will.
Thus, Christian delight in obedience finds both its pattern and its foundation in Jesus Christ.

IV. Pneumatological Transformation: The Spirit Renews Human Desire
A. The Human Heart and the Problem of Disordered Desire
According to biblical anthropology, humanity's fallen condition involves more than isolated acts of disobedience.

Sin affects the orientation of human desire.
Human beings frequently seek ultimate satisfaction in things incapable of providing it: wealth, recognition, pleasure, authority, or personal achievement.
These created goods are not necessarily evil in themselves.
The theological problem arises when finite goods become substitutes for the infinite God.
Augustine's understanding of disordered love, commonly expressed through the concept of ordo amoris, offers a useful framework.

Human flourishing requires that love be properly ordered, with God loved supremely and created goods appreciated in their appropriate place.
The statement "I get my kicks in doing the Will of My Father in Heaven" may therefore be understood as describing a reordering of desire.
Instead of seeking ultimate satisfaction in worldly excitement, the believer discovers a deeper fulfillment in loving obedience.

B. Ezekiel 36:26–27 — The Promise of an Interior Transformation
God declares through the prophet Ezekiel:
"A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you." — Ezekiel 36:26, KJV

The following verse explains that God will put His Spirit within His people and cause them to walk in His statutes.
This passage is essential to the theology of sanctification.
Divine obedience is not merely a matter of human determination.
It involves the gracious renewal of the heart.
God transforms the inner disposition of His people so that their conduct increasingly reflects His holiness.

This is especially significant because it establishes the relationship between divine initiative and human response.
The Spirit does not abolish human responsibility.
Rather, He enables believers to exercise their wills in faithful response to God.

C. Philippians 2:13 — God Working Within the Believer
Paul writes:
"For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure." — Philippians 2:13, KJV

The Greek terms θέλειν (thelein, to will) and ἐνεργεῖν (energein, to work or act) indicate that God's gracious activity extends to both human willing and action.

The believer's growing delight in obedience is therefore not simply the product of self-discipline.
It is evidence of God's sanctifying work.
This does not eliminate effort, struggle, or the need for repentance.
Rather, it places these realities within the sustaining activity of divine grace.
The Holy Spirit transforms not only what the believer does but increasingly what the believer loves.

V. The Theology of Holy Delight: God as the Highest Good
A. Psalm 16:11 — Fullness of Joy in God's Presence
The psalmist declares:
"In thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore." — Psalm 16:11, KJV

The Hebrew term שִׂמְחוֹת (simḥot) refers to joys or gladness.
The verse places the fullness of joy in the presence of God.
It does not teach that earthly pleasures are inherently sinful.
Instead, it establishes a hierarchy of value.

God is the ultimate source of goodness, and all legitimate created joys are subordinate to Him.
The believer who finds delight in doing God's will is therefore not rejecting joy.
Rather, such a believer is seeking joy in its highest and most enduring source.

B. The Relationship Between Happiness and Holiness
Christian theology must resist the assumption that holiness and happiness are necessarily opposed.
Scripture repeatedly associates righteousness with joy.
Romans 14:17 declares:
"For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost." — KJV

The Greek word χαρά again appears as an essential characteristic of life in the Spirit.
Righteousness, peace, and joy belong together in the life of God's Kingdom.
Nevertheless, Christian joy is not a guarantee of uninterrupted pleasure, prosperity, or emotional comfort.
The Apostle Paul experienced suffering, persecution, and deprivation while continuing to rejoice in the Lord.

Thus, holy delight must be understood as a durable spiritual orientation rather than a constant emotional high.
The believer can rejoice in God's faithfulness while simultaneously experiencing grief, fatigue, uncertainty, or hardship.

VI. The Cruciform Nature of Joy: Obedience Through Sacrifice
One of the most important theological qualifications to the statement is that delight in God's will must not be confused with the pursuit of pleasurable religious experiences.
The supreme example is Jesus Christ in Gethsemane.

In Luke 22:42, Jesus prays:
"Nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done." — KJV

Christ's obedience involved genuine suffering.
His submission to the Father was not characterized by superficial excitement.
It involved costly surrender.
Hebrews 12:2 presents Jesus as enduring the cross for the joy set before Him.
The passage does not suggest that crucifixion was pleasurable.
Rather, it reveals that obedience can be sustained by a greater purpose and future joy even amid profound suffering.

This introduces the doctrine of cruciform spirituality: Christian life is shaped by the self-giving pattern of the crucified and risen Christ.
Accordingly, the phrase "I get my kicks in doing the Will of My Father" must be understood carefully.
The believer may find deep satisfaction in obedience, but obedience itself may sometimes be painful.
It may require sacrifice, patience, forgiveness, self-denial, or the surrender of cherished ambitions.
True spiritual joy is therefore not dependent upon every act of obedience being emotionally rewarding.
The mature believer does not obey God only because obedience feels good; the believer learns to delight in God even when obedience is difficult.

VII. Theological Discernment: Distinguishing the Holy Spirit from Spiritual Sensationalism
The colloquial expression "high in the Spirit" requires careful theological interpretation, especially within seminary education.
The New Testament recognizes joy, spiritual gifts, worship, and powerful experiences of God's presence.
However, Scripture also commands discernment.
First John 4:1 instructs believers to test the spirits rather than accept every spiritual claim uncritically.

Similarly, 1 Corinthians 14:40 calls for worship to be conducted decently and in order.
These passages remind the Church that religious intensity is not, by itself, proof of divine activity.
A responsible theological assessment should consider several questions:
- Does the experience accord with Scripture and the Gospel of Jesus Christ?
- Does it produce humility, love, self-control, and holiness?
- Does it encourage faithful service rather than spiritual superiority?
- Does it deepen communion with God and love for one's neighbor?

The Holy Spirit's work must not be reduced to emotional stimulation.
Nor should authentic emotion be dismissed as incompatible with spiritual maturity.
Biblical spirituality accommodates both joyful expression and quiet contemplation.
It recognizes that the Spirit may work through worship, prayer, sacramental life, Scripture, service, repentance, and faithful endurance.

VIII. The Trinitarian Structure of Christian Obedience
The statement also reveals a deeply Trinitarian orientation.
The believer rejoices in the Holy Spirit while seeking to accomplish the will of the heavenly Father.
Yet the entire relationship is mediated through Jesus Christ.
The Christian life is therefore properly understood as life:
From the Father, through the Son, and in the Holy Spirit.

A. The Father: The Source and Goal
The Father's will expresses His holy purposes for creation and redemption.
Christian obedience is directed toward honoring Him.

B. The Son: The Perfect Pattern and Mediator
Jesus Christ perfectly fulfills the Father's will.
Through His death and resurrection, believers are reconciled to God and brought into a new relationship of grace.

C. The Holy Spirit: The Sanctifying Presence
The Spirit unites believers to Christ, renews their desires, empowers their obedience, and produces spiritual fruit.
This Trinitarian structure prevents the believer from treating spiritual experience as an isolated end.
The purpose of life in the Spirit is not merely to feel spiritually elevated.
It is to glorify the Father through conformity to Jesus Christ.

IX. Ethical Implications: Joy Expressed Through Love and Service
The authenticity of spiritual joy must be demonstrated in everyday Christian conduct.
Jesus declares in John 14:15:
"If ye love me, keep my commandments." — KJV

Love and obedience are inseparable within Johannine theology.
The believer who delights in the Father's will should increasingly demonstrate that delight through practical righteousness.
Such obedience includes honesty in personal affairs, compassion toward the vulnerable, forgiveness toward those who offend, responsible stewardship, and faithfulness in ordinary duties.
James 1:22 likewise calls believers to be doers of the Word rather than hearers only.

A spiritually joyful life is therefore not merely inward.
It becomes visible through the ethical quality of one's relationships and actions.
The greatest evidence of delighting in God is not the intensity of religious language but the growing manifestation of Christlike love.

X. Eschatological Fulfillment: From Present Joy to Eternal Communion
The believer's present experience of spiritual joy anticipates a greater future fulfillment.
Christian eschatology teaches that communion with God, now experienced through faith and the Spirit, will reach its consummation in the renewed creation.
Revelation 21:3–4 presents the vision of God dwelling with His people, with death, mourning, crying, and pain ultimately overcome.

This future hope places present spiritual delight within the larger history of redemption.
The joy of the Holy Spirit is a foretaste of the life to come.
Yet it remains a foretaste rather than the fullness itself.
The believer still experiences weakness, temptation, suffering, and the limitations of mortal existence.
The Spirit's presence provides hope and assurance while directing the believer toward the promised consummation of God's Kingdom.

Thus, the statement may be understood eschatologically:
The believer begins to experience now, through grace, the joy of communion with God that will find its fullness in the age to come.

XI. Ecclesiological and Formational Implications for Tenio Theological University
Within the theological and spiritual formation envisioned for Tenio Theological University, this statement offers an important educational principle:
The purpose of theological formation is not merely to increase knowledge about God but to cultivate a life that increasingly delights in knowing, loving, and obeying Him.

A mature theological education must integrate intellectual understanding, spiritual formation, ethical conduct, and practical service.
This requires a balance between rigorous biblical study and personal devotion.
Knowledge without love can produce pride.
Religious enthusiasm without discernment can produce confusion.
Obedience without an understanding of grace can deteriorate into legalism.
Theologically grounded spiritual formation seeks to avoid these distortions by placing Christ at the center of the believer's life.

For TTU, the statement may be developed into three formative principles.
1. Spiritual joy must be grounded in biblical truth.
Students should understand that authentic spiritual life is shaped by Scripture, not merely by emotional experience.
2. Obedience must arise from love and grace.
Christian discipleship is not a mechanism for earning salvation but a grateful response to God's redemptive work.
3. Spiritual maturity must produce service.
The believer's delight in God's will should be expressed through humility, compassion, justice, and faithfulness toward others.
The highest goal of theological education is therefore not personal distinction or religious prestige.
It is the formation of faithful servants whose lives bear witness to Jesus Christ.

XII. Doctrinal Synthesis: The Relationship Between Joy, Grace, and Obedience
The theological dimensions of the statement may be summarized as follows.
Theological dimension	Biblical foundation	Doctrinal significance
Fullness of the Spirit	        Ephesians 5:18	        Continuing life under the Spirit's influence
Delight in God's will	        Psalm 40:8	                Transformation of the believer's affections
Christlike obedience	        John 4:34	                The Son as the perfect model of devotion
Sanctifying grace	                Philippians 2:13	        God renews both willing and acting
Spiritual joy	                        Romans 14:17	        Joy as a characteristic of Kingdom life
Sacrificial faithfulness	Luke 22:42	                Obedience sustained even through suffering
Eternal fulfillment	        Revelation 21:3–4	The consummation of communion with God

These doctrines reveal a coherent theological progression.
Divine grace initiates the believer's transformation.
The Holy Spirit renews the inner life.
Renewed desires increasingly incline the believer toward obedience.
Obedience becomes an expression of love.
Love produces deeper communion with God.
And communion with God becomes the enduring source of Christian joy.
This progression should not be understood as a rigid sequence of spiritual achievements, but as interconnected dimensions of the life of grace.

XIII. Conclusion: The Soul That Finds Its Joy in the Father's Will
The declaration, "I get High in the Spirit of the Lord, and I get my kicks in doing the Will of My Father in Heaven," expresses, in unconventional language, a profoundly biblical aspiration.
It is the aspiration of a soul that no longer wishes to make worldly pleasure its highest pursuit.
It is the confession of a believer who desires to discover satisfaction in communion with God and faithfulness to His will.

Yet the statement reaches its deepest theological meaning when interpreted through the life of Jesus Christ.
Christ is the One whose nourishment was to accomplish the Father's work.
Christ is the One who obeyed even unto death.
Christ is the One whose resurrection establishes the foundation of Christian hope.
And through the Holy Spirit, believers are invited to share in a life increasingly conformed to His example.

The believer's greatest spiritual fulfillment is therefore not found in extraordinary experiences, personal recognition, or material success.
It is found in belonging to Christ and learning to love what God loves.
The joy of the Holy Spirit does not lead the believer away from responsibility.
It leads toward deeper faithfulness.
It does not remove every burden.
It gives strength to bear burdens in love.
It does not promise perpetual exhilaration.
It offers a deeper and more enduring hope rooted in the faithfulness of God.

XIV. Final Theological Synthesis
The statement may be expressed in a more formal theological confession:
Through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ and the sanctifying presence of the Holy Spirit, my soul discovers its deepest joy in communion with the living God. As the Spirit renews my heart, I increasingly desire to fulfill the will of my heavenly Father, not merely from obligation, but from love. For the greatest satisfaction of the redeemed soul is to glorify God through a life of faithful obedience.

The central theological principle is this:
The highest joy of the Christian life is not merely to experience the blessings of God, but to love God Himself and to find delight in doing His will.
This is the joy of a Spirit-filled life.
This is the freedom of a heart transformed by grace.
This is the spiritual satisfaction that transcends the passing pleasures of the world.

And this is the pattern of discipleship revealed perfectly in Jesus Christ.
For the soul that loves the Father, obedience becomes an offering of love; for the heart renewed by the Spirit, holiness becomes a cherished desire; and for the believer united to Christ, communion with God becomes the greatest treasure of all.
Soli Deo Gloria — To God Alone Be the Glory.

TTU Exegetical Note: The original expression is devotional and metaphorical rather than a direct biblical quotation. Its theological validity depends upon interpreting spiritual exhilaration as joy grounded in the Holy Spirit, and delight in obedience as a response to grace rather than a means of earning salvation. This distinction preserves both the expressive character of the original statement and the doctrinal integrity of Christian teaching.
