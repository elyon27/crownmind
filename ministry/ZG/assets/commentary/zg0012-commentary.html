For the Glory Alone: Divine Sovereignty and Human Agency in the Outworking of God’s Will

A Theological Commentary

A TTU Seminary-Level Exegetical Commentary

I. Introduction: A Call to Action Under Divine Purpose
The exhortation, “Make things happen for the glory of God!” appears, at first glance, to emphasize human initiative. Yet, upon deeper theological reflection, it reveals a dynamic interplay between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. It is a summons not to autonomous action, but to participatory obedience—where human effort becomes an instrument of divine intention.

This phrase must be rightly understood within a theocentric framework: God is the ultimate source, sustainer, and end of all things, and human action is meaningful only insofar as it is aligned with His glory (δόξα).

II. The Glory of God as the Supreme End
A. The Teleological Center of All Creation
Scripture consistently affirms that the glory of God is the ultimate purpose of all existence:

“For from Him and through Him and to Him are all things. To Him be glory forever” (Romans 11:36).

The phrase “for the glory of God” establishes a teleological absolute—every action, intention, and outcome must ultimately point back to God’s honor, majesty, and revealed character.

B. The Meaning of Glory (δόξα)
In biblical theology, δόξα encompasses:

- The visible manifestation of God’s attributes
- The recognition of His worth and supremacy
- The radiance of His being revealed in creation and redemption

To act for God’s glory, therefore, is to live in such a way that God’s character is made known and magnified.

III. Human Agency: “Make Things Happen” Reconsidered
A. Avoiding the Error of Autonomous Activism
The imperative “make things happen” can be misunderstood as promoting self-reliance or human-centered achievement. However, Scripture redefines human action:

“Apart from Me you can do nothing” (John 15:5). Thus, any authentic “making happen” must be:

- Dependent on divine grace
- Guided by divine will
- Empowered by the Holy Spirit

B. Instrumental Causality in Theology
Theologically, humans function as secondary causes—agents through whom God accomplishes His purposes.

“For it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for His good pleasure” (Philippians 2:13).

This establishes a paradox: We are called to act. Yet God is the ultimate actor within our action
Thus, “making things happen” becomes cooperation with divine activity, not competition with it.

IV. Christological Model: Active Obedience for the Father’s Glory
Christ Himself embodies this principle perfectly:

“I glorified You on earth, having accomplished the work that You gave Me to do” (John 17:4).

Key elements of Christ’s model:

- Initiative rooted in obedience
- Action aligned with the Father’s will
- Outcome directed toward divine glory

Thus, the believer’s call to action is a participation in the active obedience of Christ.

V. Pneumatological Empowerment: The Spirit as the Agent of Effectiveness
The ability to “make things happen” for God’s glory is mediated through the Holy Spirit:

“You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be My witnesses” (Acts 1:8).

The Spirit provides:

- Power (δύναμις) for action
- Wisdom for discernment
- Boldness for mission

Without the Spirit, human effort remains ineffective in achieving eternal significance.

VI. Ethical and Missional Implications
A. Action as Worship
Every act done for God’s glory becomes an act of worship:

“Whatever you do, do all to the glory of God” (1 Corinthians 10:31).

Thus:

- Work becomes worship
- Service becomes sacrifice
- Mission becomes manifestation of divine purpose

B. Missional Urgency
To “make things happen” also implies intentional engagement in God’s mission:

- Proclaiming the Gospel
- Serving others in love
- Advancing righteousness and justice

This reflects the Great Commission (Matthew 28:18–20), where believers are called to active participation in God’s redemptive work.

VII. The Tension Between Divine Sovereignty and Human Responsibility
This phrase sits within a classical theological tension:

Divine Sovereignty	Human Responsibility
God ordains all things	Humans are called to act
God empowers	        Humans obey
God receives glory	Humans participate

Rather than contradiction, this is a mystery of cooperation—God’s sovereignty establishes the certainty of His purposes, while human responsibility defines the means by which those purposes unfold.

VIII. Spiritual Theology: The Discipline of God-Centered Action. To live out this exhortation requires:

- Discernment – knowing what aligns with God’s will
- Dependence – relying on divine strength
- Diligence – committing to faithful action
- Detachment – relinquishing personal glory

This guards against two extremes:

- Passivity (“God will do everything”)
- Prideful activism (“I will accomplish everything”)

The proper posture is active dependence.

IX. Eschatological Orientation: Lasting Impact vs. Temporal Achievement
Not all “things that happen” have eternal value. The measure is not activity but eternal significance:

“Each one’s work will become manifest… the fire will test what sort of work each one has done” (1 Corinthians 3:13).

Thus, actions done for God’s glory:

- Endure beyond time
- Participate in eternal realities
- Contribute to the coming Kingdom

X. Ecclesiological and Formational Implications (TTU Context)

Within Tenio Theological University (TTU), this phrase becomes a guiding principle for formation:
Students are trained to act purposefully and missionally. Action is grounded in theological clarity and spiritual dependence
The goal is to produce leaders who execute vision for God’s glory, not personal ambition. Thus, formation integrates:

- Thought (theology)
- Being (spirituality)
- Doing (mission)

XI. Conclusion: Action That Reflects Divine Glory
The exhortation ultimately calls for a life where:

- God is the source of all action
- God is the means sustaining the action
- God is the end for which the action exists

Final Theological Synthesis

To declare, “Make things happen for the glory of God!” is to affirm:

- That human action is meaningful only in alignment with divine purpose
- That God works through willing and obedient vessels
- That all outcomes must ultimately magnify His glory

It is not a call to self-driven achievement, but to Spirit-empowered participation in the purposes of God.

Act, but in dependence.
Labor, but in submission.
Accomplish, but for His glory alone.
